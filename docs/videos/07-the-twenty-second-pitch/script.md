# 07 · The twenty-second pitch

You have twenty-five days to find a hundred cooks, and every one of those conversations starts the same way: twenty seconds, in plain words. This video gives you the pitch line by line, then shows how it changes at four doors: people you know, cooks already posting on Instagram, the shop counter, and church.

## The pitch

Here it is in full. Say it out loud until it sounds like you and not like a script.

Hi, I'm building Sweet Hand, a free site where people in Toronto find Caribbean home cooks by dish, island and neighbourhood. It opens at the end of November, right before Christmas orders. It's free to list, you set your own menu, batches and days, and your home address never shows, only your area. The first hundred cooks get a free photo shoot of their dishes in my studio downtown. Can I put you on the list?

That's about seventy words. At a normal speaking pace it lands in twenty seconds, and it answers every question a cook is about to ask before they ask it.

## Line by line

Line one says who you are and what it is. Three words do the work: free, find, and neighbourhood. A cook hears that people who have never heard of them could find them, and that it costs nothing.

Line two says when. End of November, right before Christmas orders. Christmas is the reason to say yes today instead of next month. Black cake fruit is soaking right now, and sorrel orders open in November. Nobody wants to miss December.

Line three answers the three worries before they come up. Free to list. You set your own menu, batches and days. Your home address never shows. Say all three every time, even to someone who seems relaxed. The privacy line is the one people repeat to their friends.

Line four is the gift. A free photo shoot of their dishes, for the first hundred. It's real, it's valuable, and it's limited. Don't inflate it. It's about ninety minutes in the studio, they bring the food, and they keep the photos for their own Instagram.

Line five is the ask. Can I put you on the list? That is a yes or no question. Don't end with "what do you think", because what people think is "maybe". Ask for the list.

## After the yes

When they say yes, two more questions make the number.

First: which dishes should I write down? Use their words for the dish and the tradition. Trini, Guyanese, Bajan, Jamaican, Haitian. Never just "Caribbean".

Second: who else cooks that I should talk to? Then: can I say you sent me? That second question is the engine of the whole plan. A warm introduction says yes about half the time. A cold message says yes about one time in seven. Ask it of every single cook, without exception.

## What you never say

Three things stay out of your mouth at every door.

Never say that Sweet Hand certifies, inspects or approves anyone's kitchen. It doesn't. A cook writes their own food safety note in their own words, or writes nothing.

Never say that a listing is a licence, or anything like one.

Never promise orders. You can promise to be found. You can't promise who buys.

And say what exists today. The site is a launch list now, and a directory on November twenty-sixth. Show the preview and the video for what it will be, and say the word "will".

## People you know

The first door is the one you're sitting at today: Thanksgiving. Family, friends, church friends, the people you've photographed.

Don't pitch here. Ask about buying. Who makes your black cake? Who did the food at the last christening? Who do you order roti from? Write every name down. Expect ten to twenty names from this weekend alone.

With the cooks they name, the pitch shrinks to one line because the trust is already there. Your cousin gave me your number and said you make the best pelau in Ajax. I'm building a free site so people find cooks like you by dish and neighbourhood. Can I put you on the list?

Then the two questions. With people you know, ask for three names instead of two.

## Cooks already posting

The second door is Instagram and TikTok. These cooks are already selling to strangers. They post a plate and say "D M to order". Your pitch is that Sweet Hand is the search page they don't have.

In a direct message the pitch becomes three lines. The first line names their dish, so they know you actually looked. Your oxtail looks serious. The second line is the one-liner and the gift: a free directory of Caribbean home cooks in the G T A, opening before Christmas, free to list, address stays private, and the first hundred cooks get a free studio shoot. The third line asks for five minutes on the phone or a move to WhatsApp.

Never try to close inside Instagram. A yes almost never happens in a D M. Send twenty to thirty new messages a day, follow and like a post before each one, and when someone replies, answer within the hour.

## The shop counter

The third door is the Caribbean grocer, the roti shop, the bakery, the salon and the barbershop. Here you're not pitching a cook. You're pitching the person who knows the cooks.

Hand over a business card and use one sentence. I'm building a free directory of Caribbean home cooks. Do you know people who sell from home? Owners know, and they usually like the idea, because the cooks are their best customers.

Then three small asks. Can I leave a stack of flyers at the till? Can I put a sticker on the door or the board? Can I look at your bulletin board? Photograph the board. Every handwritten "black cake orders" card is a lead with a phone number on it.

Then hit the salon and the barbershop on the same block. That's where cooks get recommended, and the owners often sell food on the side themselves.

## Church

The fourth door is church. Call the office first, in week zero. Ask for the pastor or the hospitality committee, and ask for two minutes at the announcements or a table after the service.

At the table the pitch is the full version, with one change: start with the kitchen. The people who run the church kitchen know every cook in the congregation. Ask them first, and ask them to walk you to the cooks.

It's harvest festival and bazaar season. Every table at a bake sale is a maker. Keep the tone right: you're asking the church for help with something that helps its own people, not selling at church.

## Things to remember

Twenty seconds, five lines: what it is, when, the three worries, the gift, the ask.

After every yes, two questions: which dishes, and who else. Ask for permission to use their name.

Never say certified, licensed or guaranteed. Say what exists today.

People you know: ask about buying first, then one line. Instagram: three lines, then move to a call. The shop: pitch the owner, not the cook. Church: start with the kitchen.
