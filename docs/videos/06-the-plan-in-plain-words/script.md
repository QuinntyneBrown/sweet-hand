# 06 · The plan, in plain words

Imagine a big picture book of every Caribbean home cook in Toronto and the GTA. You open it, you search for doubles in Scarborough or pepperpot in Brampton, and you find someone with a sweet hand. That book is Sweet Hand. It's free for cooks to be in, and free for you to look.

So how does a free book pay the bills? This video explains the business plan in plain words, as of October 2026. At the end, we look at one big what-if: what changes if the founder gets a food handler certificate and starts cooking on Sweet Hand too.

## The problem

About three hundred and seventy-five thousand people in the GTA have Caribbean roots. About two thousand five hundred home cooks sell food. And there is no book that lists them.

Today, you find a cook through a cousin, a church group or a WhatsApp chat. That means each cook only sells to people who already know them. Sweet Hand fixes that. A cook gets found by dish, by island and by neighbourhood, by people who have never heard of them.

And it isn't only for people with Caribbean roots. Jerk, roti, doubles and patties sell across the whole city.

## Four jars

Think of the money as four jars. Listing and searching stay free, whatever jar we're talking about.

The first jar is the website. Cooks who want more can pay twenty-nine dollars a month for Maker Pro. Cooks can pay to be featured. When an order or a catering job comes through Sweet Hand, Sweet Hand keeps ten percent. And sponsors, like Caribbean grocers, pay to be seen.

The second jar is the camera. The founder is a photographer. Food shoots start at two hundred and forty-nine dollars for ten dishes. Then there are headshots, short videos and weddings.

The third jar is the room. The founder can use a downtown studio for one hundred dollars a day. It hosts supper clubs, pop-up markets and cooking classes, where a cook teaches and the class pays.

The fourth jar is the computer. The founder builds software. Cooks can get their own website for twenty-five dollars a month. Caterers can get custom sites and order forms. And later, other cities can rent the whole Sweet Hand system for their own cuisines.

The jars feed each other. A cook books a shoot. The photos make their page look great. The page brings orders. The orders make Maker Pro worth paying for.

## Year by year

The order matters.

In year one, the camera and the computer pay the bills while the book fills up with cooks. The plan says about ninety-eight thousand dollars.

In year two, the website starts to catch up, and the plan says about three hundred thousand.

In year three, the website is the biggest part, about half of roughly five hundred and sixty-five thousand dollars. That matters, because website money doesn't need the founder's hours. A photo shoot does.

Costs stay small: about twenty-two thousand dollars in year one. And the careful version of the plan? Take a bit more than half of every number.

## The first ninety days

Everything points at one date: launch day, November twenty-sixth, right as people start ordering black cake and sorrel for Christmas.

Before then, the first one hundred cooks get a free food shoot in the studio. In return, they fill in their page and share it on launch day. That costs about three thousand dollars in studio days, and it gives Sweet Hand the best Caribbean food photos in the city.

After ninety days, the plan checks six targets: one hundred cooks live, five hundred eaters signed up, five thousand dollars of Christmas pre-orders, ten paid shoots, one sold-out supper club and three sponsor conversations. Miss two or more, and some jars get dropped.

## One person

All of this is one person's job, so the plan guards the calendar. Shoots are batched, three or four cooks to a studio day. Payments and deliveries run on their own. Weddings and big custom apps are capped. Even then, year one fills about two hundred and thirty-two of roughly two hundred and forty working days.

And there's a list of things the founder never does: cook, deliver, check kitchens, chase payments by hand, or build something no paying cook asked for.

## The biggest risk

The biggest risk is the food rules. In Ontario, a home cook who makes only low-risk food, like bread, jam or chocolate, gets lighter rules. Jerk, oxtail, curry and roti with meat are not low-risk. Those cooks need a certified food handler, they tell public health, and they can be inspected.

So Sweet Hand stays a book, not a seller. The cook is the seller. What a cook says about their kitchen is shown as the cook's own statement. Sweet Hand never vouches for a kitchen.

## What if the founder cooks too?

Now the what-if. Remember the never list? It starts with cook. Here's what changes if the founder gets a food handler certificate and lists a kitchen on Sweet Hand, as cook number one hundred and one.

First, what it takes. The certificate comes from a course of about seven hours. As of October 2026, George Brown College runs one online for about one hundred and thirty dollars, and passing it leads to a certificate from Toronto Public Health. Then the kitchen has to be reported to public health. For food like jerk or curry, the full rules apply, including a separate hand-washing sink. The lighter path is to cook only low-risk things, like bread.

What it adds. The founder learns Sweet Hand from the cook's side: pre-orders, batch limits and sold-out days, all tested for real. Every batch is a story and a photo shoot. And cooks trust someone who has done what they do.

There's some money too, but this is our own rough guess, not the plan's. One batch weekend a month, twenty to thirty orders at fifteen to twenty-five dollars, might leave two to five thousand dollars a year after ingredients.

What it costs. Time, first. The calendar is already full, and one studio day of food shoots earns more than a whole batch weekend. Second, fairness. The founder controls the search results, the featured spots and the verified badge, so other cooks need to see the founder playing by the same rules. Third, safety. The plan protects Sweet Hand by making the cook the seller. When the cook is the founder, that line blurs, so the cooking should sit in its own business with its own insurance. Ask a lawyer and an insurance broker.

So here's the verdict. Get the certificate either way. It's cheap, it builds trust, and it's the same kind of course Sweet Hand will point cooks to. If the founder lists a kitchen, keep it small and clearly labelled: one batch a month, no paid spots, no special treatment. Keep never cook at scale in the plan. Then let the ninety-day review decide if it earned its place.

## Things to remember

- Sweet Hand is free to list and free to search.
- The money comes from four jars: the website, the camera, the room and the computer.
- The camera and the computer pay first. The website pays most by year three.
- Sweet Hand is a book, not a seller. The cook is the seller.
- If the founder cooks, keep it small, labelled and separate.
